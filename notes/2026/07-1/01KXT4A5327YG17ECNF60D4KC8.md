*ISE Canon — the core concepts of ISE and how it works. A living reference; concepts are added as they are agreed.*

## The two layers: Signals and Incidents

Everything ISE reacts to falls into one of two layers, and keeping them separate is the central idea.

- **Signals** are *transient* and *machine-owned*. They reflect the current state of a system and come and go on their own as that state changes. There are two kinds — **Alerts** and **Observations**. Nobody has to "deal with" a signal; it is simply present or not.
- An **Incident** is *durable* and *human-owned*. It is the unit of work. Once opened it must be managed through to resolution by a person — it does not disappear just because the signals underneath it did.

The point of the Incident is to **disambiguate the transience of signals**. Alerts and Observations may flap; the Incident holds steady so a human has one stable thing to work, with a full history of what happened underneath it.

A guiding principle sits behind the two kinds of signal: **ISE defers detection to a source when that source has its own detection layer, and detects for itself only where the source does not.** DataDog has monitors, so ISE trusts them (Alerts). Kubernetes has no equivalent, so ISE forms its own judgement there (Observations).

## Record types

### Alert
A pre-defined failure state detected by an integration's *own* monitoring, which has decided a threshold or condition was breached. ISE forwards it rather than re-deriving it. Alerts are created in ISE with the details the integration provides.

- **Intake:** fast / event-driven — read from the integration as its state is polled, so an Alert surfaces as quickly as possible.
- **Identity & dedup:** keyed on the source's **stable identity plus trigger group** — for DataDog, `monitor/{id}/{group}`, never the display name (names collide and can be edited). A *multi-alert* monitor (one definition grouped e.g. `by host`) produces **one Alert per triggering group**: "Agent not responding on Server X" and "…on Server Y" are two distinct Alerts. The group key doubles as the affected-infrastructure entity for the estate knowledge base. A history of occurrences (last triggered, recurrences) is kept on the record.
- **Carries** the source's severity, mapped onto the canonical ladder (see *Severity, confidence & incident thresholds*).
- **Status:**
  - **Triggered** — currently firing.
  - **Recurring** — has triggered before within its lifetime window (lifetime configurable in settings).
  - **Recovered** — stopped firing on its own.
  - **Ignored** — a known false/unwanted alert; occurrences are still recorded but it stays suppressed and never opens or reactivates an Incident.
  - **Resolved** — *derived*, never set directly (see *Signal resolution & suppression*).

### Observation
An anomaly or issue ISE itself detects while checking an integration that has no detection layer of its own. This is ISE's *opinion*, so it is inherently lower-precision than an Alert and carries an ISE **confidence** ("how sure") alongside its severity ("how bad").

- **Intake:** the **Obs Loop** (below), on a slow schedule — and additionally gathered on demand while investigating an Incident.
- **Dedup:** by event *and* affected infrastructure (the estate entity); a record per observation keeps a history of when it was seen.
- **Carries** an ISE-assigned canonical severity and a confidence (see *Severity, confidence & incident thresholds*).
- **Status:**
  - **New** — detected for the first time.
  - **Recurring** — seen before within its lifetime window (configurable in settings).
  - **Recovered** — went away on its own / not seen again.
  - **Ignored** — known and unwanted in the queue; still recorded, never opens or reactivates an Incident.
  - **Resolved** — *derived*, never set directly (see below).

### Signal resolution & suppression
A signal's **Resolved is never set directly** — it is *derived* by cascade when its Incident is resolved, so "fixed" has a single source of truth.

- **Normal path:** signals go green (Recovered) → a human Resolves the Incident → the signals read Resolved.
- **Early-resolve path:** a human Resolves an Incident while its signals are *still firing* → those signals are forced to Resolved too.
- **Re-assertion:** if a Resolved signal is reported again, that is a fresh trigger → it **Reactivates** the Incident and the signal returns to Triggered/Recurring.
- **Escape hatch:** to stop a persistent/known signal from reopening, set the *signal* to **Ignored** — the durable suppression.

Consequence: a *continuously* firing signal will bounce straight back after an early-resolve (it re-asserts on the next poll), so early-resolve only "sticks" against a persistent signal if it is also Ignored. This is intended. The only two direct human actions on a signal are **Ignore** and adjusting its severity (see below); everything else about a signal is machine-driven.

### Incident
A unique, durable investigation of related Alerts and/or Observations. The Incident is the timeline of what happened, who did what, and when.

- **Opened by:** an Alert (automatically, above the threshold); a *severe, high-confidence* Observation (automatically, above the threshold); or a person, manually, from any signal.
- **Correlation (ingest):** one signal → at most one *open* Incident. A firing Alert either opens a new Incident or attaches to the existing open one for that signal; recovered-and-re-triggered signals re-attach rather than spawning duplicates.
- **Correlation (investigation):** related signals across the estate are added during the targeted investigation. Where several Incidents turn out to share a root cause, **ISE proposes a merge and a human accepts or rejects it** — merging is never automatic, because it is a judgement that needs the estate knowledge base.
- **Resolution is human-owned:** a signal clearing is a *hint* ("Recovered"), never an automatic close.
- **Severity escalates, but never de-escalates.** *Agreed 2026-07-22.* A live Incident follows its signal *upward*: when the signal's effective severity rises above the Incident's, the Incident is re-graded and the change is recorded on its timeline. It never follows the signal back down. The asymmetry is the whole design — escalation is *information* ("the estate is worse than this record says", and saying so late is worse than saying so loudly), whereas de-escalation would be a *judgement*, and judgement about how urgent something is belongs to the operator. A symmetric rule would also reintroduce flapping through the back door: a monitor oscillating Warn↔Alert would swing the Incident's severity, and its place in the queue, on every sync — precisely what the two-layer split exists to prevent. Consequence to read correctly: an Incident's severity records **the worst the estate got** during its life, not how bad things are right now. Only an Incident nobody has stood down escalates (New / Active / Reactivated); once a human has Resolved or dismissed it, escalation must not half-undo that decision.
- **Status:**
  - **New** — newly created.
  - **Active** — assigned to a person to deal with.
  - **Resolved** — believed fixed; may reactivate if it recurs.
  - **Reactivated** — was Resolved but detected again within the reactivation window.
  - **Closed** — Resolved for more than *x* days without recurring; moves here automatically (*x* configurable). A recurrence after Closed is a *new* Incident. (The correlation key must persist on a Resolved-but-not-Closed Incident so a recurrence can reattach.)

## Severity, confidence & incident thresholds

Every signal is placed on one **canonical severity ladder**, and the decision to auto-open an Incident is made once against it — so the rung means the same thing everywhere.

The ladder: **info < low < medium < high < critical**.

- **Alerts** get their severity from a per-connector mapping of the source's native levels (DataDog: `Alert→high, Warn→medium, No Data→low`). A source's scale is a subset of the ladder — DataDog cannot natively produce `critical`, so a critical Incident can only come from an Observation or another integration. That is the point of normalising: the ladder is a superset of any one source's scale.
- **Observations** are *born* with an ISE-assigned canonical severity ("how bad") plus a **confidence** ("how sure") — two distinct axes.

**Auto-incident policy is global, tuning is local.**
- A single **global auto-incident threshold** on the ladder (e.g. ≥ High) expresses policy: "we act on High and above," true everywhere.
- Sensitivity is tuned not by moving the threshold per integration, but by a **severity-override layer** over the connector defaults: `effective severity = override ?? connector default`. Overrides are **scoped** (per integration, down to signal-type or entity) and **auditable**, and never mutate the connector default (a connector upgrade must not wipe local tuning; history shows "downgraded high→medium by <person>"). *Example: pod restarts in the g5 cluster are semi-normal, so that observation type is overridden `high→medium`; with the threshold at ≥ High it no longer auto-opens Incidents, but is still recorded and available as evidence.* These overrides are the structured *tuning* register of the estate knowledge base (below).

**The open rule:**
- **Alerts** (high-confidence by construction): auto-open if effective severity ≥ threshold.
- **Observations**: auto-open if effective severity ≥ threshold **and** confidence ≥ a global confidence bar; otherwise they remain Observations for a human to promote.
- The threshold governs **automation only** — a human can always open an Incident manually from any signal below the bar.

**Two tools, one gradient:** *downgrade severity* = "keep showing me, keep it as evidence, just don't open an Incident for each one"; *Ignore* = "don't show me at all." Downgrade is the soft, common tuning; Ignore is the hard mute. For this to work in practice, downgrading must be a one-click action *from where the noise appears* (the Incident/Observation in the queue) — tuning as a byproduct of use, like maintaining the estate knowledge base.

## The Obs Loop

The recurring Observation check. It runs on a regular, deliberately *slow* cadence — expensive detection work belongs on a loop measured in hours/daily, not minutes. Configurable in settings: each integration can be opted in or out, and its schedule set independently.

Each pass checks an integration for unusual or anomalous behaviour, records Observations, and captures what it sees as a **baseline of record** — the "normal" that later investigations compare against. It also validates the estate knowledge base against reality, surfacing drift as low-severity Observations. It is mostly *cheap deterministic detectors* (known failure modes) plus context-driven suppression, with AI reserved for genuine novelty — not an expensive model scanning everything, which is the pattern that drains budget.

**Drift watches durable knowledge; placement churn is counted, not reported.** *(Agreed 2026-07-26, ISE-301.)* The drift check raises Observations only for durable knowledge going stale — dependency edges (`depends-on` / `routes-to`) and anything a **human asserted or confirmed**, whose divergence from reality always surfaces. Volatile placement (`runs-on`, discovery-derived `part-of`) never raises drift: on an autoscaled estate nodes recycle constantly, and discovery's set-replacement plus entity retirement already reconcile placement silently — one Observation per vanished placement edge is noise by construction (it once produced 528 of 549 open findings). The churn is still **counted**, not ignored — read as a *rate* straight from Kubernetes' own event aggregation, so no churn store is needed (ISE-301 part 2): a **node-flap detector** raises one high Observation for a still-present node bouncing Ready↔NotReady past a threshold (a cleanly recycled node's object is gone from the cluster, so normal Karpenter churn can never read as a flap), and repeated `FailedScheduling`/`Unhealthy` events escalate from a records-only note to a page past a churn threshold. A generic pod-reschedule rate is deliberately **not** alerted on — consolidation reschedules pods as normal operation, so a raw rate cannot be non-noisy without baselines; faults are caught at their root instead (ADR 0030).

## The Incident Loop

Once an Incident is open, it is worked through a single loop that both *uses* what ISE has learned and *adds* to it. This closed feedback loop is what makes ISE cheaper and sharper with use; it extends the propose → approve → execute remediation loop with a learning bookend at each end.

1. **Raised** — from an Alert, a severe/high-confidence Observation, or a person.
2. **Recall** — cheap retrieval: match the Incident against **memory** (past incident instances with a similar signature/entity — "this looks like last month's connection-pool exhaustion on Kora") and **playbooks** (distilled procedures for this class of problem). Reframes the work from "solve from scratch" to "verify a prior."
3. **Investigate the real state** *(collaborative)* — gather current evidence, directed by knowledge-base traversal and any matched playbook, and test the recalled hypotheses against reality. A prior is a hypothesis to verify, never an answer to run.
4. **Propose resolution** *(collaborative)* — recommend a remediation via propose → approve → execute; default-deny, human-approved (ADR 0017/0021), refinable in conversation (ADR 0024).
5. **Validate resolution** *(collaborative)* — confirm the fix worked. This is the *same mechanism* as the Incident status model: validation confirms enough to **Resolve**; the reactivation window catches regressions; Closed-after-*x*-days-quiet is final validation.
6. **Update the knowledge base** — write back what was learned: the incident instance (automatic history, including *failed* attempts, which are as valuable as successes), any newly-discovered structure/context, and a **playbook** distillation (AI-proposed, human-confirmed — a bad playbook is confidently-wrong advice feeding remediation). Feeds the next Incident's Recall.

**Self-tiering.** Cost and human effort scale with novelty, automatically: a strong memory/playbook match short-circuits — quick confirm, high-confidence proposal, a rubber-stamp approval — while a novel Incident goes deep and heavily collaborative. Compute is spent in proportion to how new the problem is, and **cost per Incident falls as memory grows** — the inverse of a cold-start-every-time system.

**Two failure modes designed against:** *over-fitting to memory* (a new cause wearing a familiar signature) — priors must be rejectable by the investigation, which is explicitly prompted to consider "what if this isn't the usual cause?"; and *human fatigue* — mitigated by the self-tiering above, asking for proportionate involvement.

The three collaborative steps run over the existing chat surface (ADR 0022/0024). What is genuinely new versus today's diagnose → propose → execute → verify is the two bookends — **Recall** at the front and **Update** at the back — which wrap the existing remediation loop in a learning layer rather than replacing it.

### Incident signatures — how Recall matches
Recall turns on a signature, and there are *two*, computed at different moments for different jobs:

- **Trigger signature** — computed at Recall, cheaply, from the signal identity (`monitor/{id}/{group}`, or observation type) + the affected entity/entity-type. Coarse; its job is *retrieval*.
- **Diagnostic signature** — computed after Investigate, from the *cause found* (the failure mode). Precise; its job is *learning* — memory and playbooks are keyed on it.

The split is load-bearing because trigger↔cause is **many-to-many**: one trigger ("agent not responding") can have several causes; one cause (a DB outage) trips many triggers. So Recall matches on the trigger to get into the neighbourhood, and the diagnostic signatures of the priors it retrieves say which causes to check first.

- **Build signatures from stable attributes** (signal type, entity identity, failure mode), never volatile ones (event ids, timestamps, exact counts, time-varying title text). This is the `findings_fingerprint` lesson (ISE-44) — that fingerprint deliberately excludes `last_seen_at`; a volatile signature either never matches (no learning) or matches on noise (false learning).
- **Graded, structured-first matching.** Ranked similarity with a confidence, not an equality test: deterministic structured filters (same signal type + entity type + topology shape) narrow the set, semantic similarity ranks within it. Structured-first keeps Recall cheap and — because a human validates the recall — explainable.
- **Hung off the entity graph.** The backbone is `(signal-type, entity)`, so memory attaches to entities in the knowledge base (Kora carries its own incident history) and Recall pulls priors off the same graph it traverses for evidence — no separate index to keep in sync.
- **Layered by specificity and recency.** Match exact (this signal on this entity) → entity-general (this signal type on this entity type) → class (this failure mode anywhere), weighted by specificity and recency (the estate changes; an old match is a weak hint). Playbooks key on the coarse (class) level; memory on the fine (instance) level.
- **Loose but verified.** The dial runs from loose-match/false-recall to tight-match/no-learning; resolve it by biasing to *loose recall for consideration* and letting Investigate reject. Generous matching is safe because recall never acts — only the human-approved proposal does.

### Playbooks
A playbook is the distilled, reusable form of "how we handle this class of problem" — a *promoted, human-blessed generalisation* over incident memory (which is raw instances, captured automatically).

**Anatomy** (six parts):
1. **Applicability** — the class-level signature (+ optional context conditions) that decides when it fires; what Recall matches against.
2. **Ranked hypotheses** — likely causes and how to tell them apart; turns a coarse trigger match into a directed investigation.
3. **Investigation plan** — the ordered evidence to gather, as *intents* not literal calls. This field *is* the scoped evidence plan that replaces the blind tool loop.
4. **Remediation options** — candidate fixes, each tied to a cause and each a **reference to a governed action** from the connector's `action_catalogue()` (`ActionSpec`, with tier and parameters).
5. **Validation criteria** — how to confirm success; feeds the loop's Validate step.
6. **Efficacy & provenance** — authored-by / derived-from, a live success/failure record, last-used, confidence.

**Structured where it touches governance, narrative where it touches reasoning.** Structured: applicability, action references, validation (matching and governance must be machine-readable). Narrative: hypotheses and evidence intents (guidance the AI reasons with, not a rigid tree). A playbook is **loaded as context that shapes investigation and proposal — never a script the system runs**; "prior to verify" applies at the playbook level.

**Steps are stable intent, resolved at run time.** The investigation plan encodes stable intent ("check node memory pressure on the host running the agent") and lets the AI + estate graph + connector resolve the *how* now — the stable-vs-volatile lesson, so the playbook survives estate churn.

**Judgment, not privilege** *(the key safety property)*. A playbook's remediation options reference the *existing* action catalogue — it introduces no new execution capability and can never propose anything the catalogue + approval gate wouldn't already allow. Governance (default-deny, tiered approval — ADR 0017/0021) is unchanged. Playbooks make ISE smarter about *proposing*, never more powerful about *executing*.

**Advisory playbooks earn standing from feedback.** *(Agreed 2026-07-26, ISE-303.)* A playbook distilled from a diagnosis alone — hypotheses, no catalogue operation — is *advisory*: the executed-op efficacy rule can never score it. It earns standing through a separate feedback ledger (never conflated with proven-fix efficacy): an operator's one-click helped / didn't-apply verdict from Recall (always wins), plus a conservative, positive-only automatic credit when a resolved incident's recorded diagnosis bears its hypothesis out. Recall ranks a remediation playbook by executed efficacy and an advisory one by its feedback standing; helped-false is the advisory anti-rot signal.

**Authoring — crystallised incidents.** Three sources: distilled from a resolved incident (primary/organic — Update proposes, human confirms); authored directly (seed a known class); refined by repetition (each match adds causes, reorders by frequency, updates efficacy). Maturity grows with successful reuse: resolution → candidate → confirmed → matured.

**Efficacy is the anti-rot immune system.** Each application records its outcome (did the hypothesis hold? did remediation pass validation?). Ranking uses the live success rate; a falling rate flags the playbook for review and can surface like drift ("worked 8/10, last three failed since the DB moved to Aurora"). Failed applications are as valuable as successes. Stored in Postgres, versioned with provenance so a bad edit is auditable and reversible.

## Applying the model: DataDog (worked example)

DataDog already runs the "is something wrong?" evaluation continuously, over data ISE never ingests. So:

- **Monitors → Alerts.** Only monitor state produces Alerts, keyed by `monitor/{id}/{group}` so multi-alert monitors split correctly per scope.
- **Metrics, events, logs, dashboards, service map → not issue sources.** They are **evidence, gathered on demand** during an Incident investigation to explain *why* a monitor tripped.

This avoids duplicating detection DataDog has already done and avoids surfacing signals DataDog itself doesn't consider alert-worthy. Contrast Kubernetes: no monitor layer, so ISE detects there itself → Observations.

## Applying the model: AWS (v1 — read-only)

*Decided 2026-07-29 (sprint AWS Integration, ISE-358..363; ADR 0058 to be written with the foundation task).*

- **One Integration instance per AWS account**, authenticated with **static access keys** in the existing envelope-encrypted credential store (a keyless OIDC / Roles-Anywhere path was considered and rejected for now). The platform's read/write credential split maps onto two IAM identities; the write side is reserved for the follow-on actions sprint. v1 declares **Alerts + Entities + Evidence** — the action catalogue is empty.
- **Resources become estate entities:** EC2, RDS, EKS, ELB/ALB, S3, with **account-scoped native keys** (`aws:{account_id}:{arn}`, the ADR 0045 lesson). Two new entity types: `load-balancer` and `bucket`; EC2→host, RDS→database, EKS→cluster. AWS resource tags feed the unified tag pool.
- **Join, don't duplicate.** AWS discovery offers cross-keys onto the entities other integrations already know — EC2 instance → `datadog:host:{instance-id}` and `k8s:node:{node-name}`; EKS cluster → `k8s:cluster:{name}` — so the tier-1 alias harvest merges AWS resources into the existing estate rather than minting a shadow one (the ISE-205 precedent).
- **AWS alerting follows the deferral principle.** CloudWatch alarms and AWS Health events are the source's own detection layer, so ISE forwards them as **Alerts** (keyed on the alarm/event ARN) riding the standard signal lifecycle. Cross-source dedupe/reinforcement is deliberately the *existing* machinery — same-entity attribution plus human-gated "same affected entity" merge candidates — not a new correlation layer. The Health API needs a Business+ support plan, so that slice degrades gracefully where absent.
- **Evidence on demand:** describe-resource, list-resources, CloudWatch metrics/logs, CloudTrail lookup — through the standard Evidence contract, lighting up every investigation surface with no per-surface wiring.

## The Estate Knowledge Base

More than a topology map: the estate model is the shared knowledge base that makes the AI investigator competent in *this specific estate* rather than generically. It carries three registers of knowledge — all of them "what the humans and the environment know that a generic tool does not":

1. **Structural** — **entities** (services, clusters, databases, hosts, namespaces…) and typed, directed **relationships** (`runs-on`, `depends-on`, `hosted-on`, `routes-to`, `part-of`). Each entity has a canonical id, a type, and a set of integration **aliases** — how each connected system names it (`datadog:service:service-x`, `k8s:g5/team-a/deploy/service-x`). Edges carry provenance and a last-confirmed timestamp. Distinct from a `System` (a connected integration instance) — estate entities live *across and inside* Systems.
2. **Contextual** — free-form annotations that change how ISE *interprets* what it sees: behavioural caveats ("this cluster runs Karpenter, expect transient nodes on demand") and business meaning/criticality ("Kora is the company CRM, used across the business, composed of X/Y/Z"). Authored, sticky, loaded as context whenever the investigator reaches that entity.
3. **Tuning** — the severity overrides and Ignores (see *Severity…*), the structured face of the same knowledge.

All three are one idea: **capturing local human knowledge so ISE behaves correctly in this estate.** They act in two registers — *structured rules* (overrides, Ignore) applied deterministically at ingest, and *narrative context* fed into the AI's reasoning during investigation and observation. Both are wanted: structured for the known-and-repetitive, narrative for judgement.

Over time the knowledge base also accumulates **incident memory** and **playbooks** through the Incident Loop — the record of what has happened in this estate and the distilled procedures for handling it. Memory and playbooks are estate knowledge like any other; the Incident Loop is the knowledge base's read/write cycle, which is why the knowledge base is maintained by *working incidents*, not by a separate process.

**Stored in Postgres, not a graph DB.** At estate scale a few tables (entities, aliases, edges, annotations) with recursive queries suffice; a graph DB would be its own ADR and ops burden and cut against ADR 0002.

**Identity resolution** is the crux — deciding two integration names are the same entity — cheapest-first:
1. **Harvest shared keys / cross-tags automatically.** Integrations often already carry each other's coordinates (a DataDog agent in K8s tags metrics with `kube_namespace`/`kube_deployment`; K8s workloads carry `tags.datadoghq.com/service`). Where a shared key exists, resolution is free.
2. **Heuristic candidate-match** where no shared key exists — the AI proposes candidates, a human confirms; never auto-merge on a guess.
3. **Human-asserted aliases** are authoritative and sticky.

**Discovered vs authored.** Existence and placement (`runs-on`, `hosted-on`) are discovered from the integrations and refreshed on a cadence. Semantic `depends-on` edges no integration knows, criticality, context annotations, and corrections are authored and sticky. On conflict, **authored wins and the conflict is surfaced** (the "operator dismissal is sticky" pattern).

**The knowledge base is a cache, and the Obs Loop validates it.** Seeded by discovery, enriched as a byproduct of investigation (resolve-once, persist), and checked by the Obs Loop — divergence from reality ("graph says Service-X runs on g5, but it is no longer there") surfaces as a low-severity Observation. The thing that keeps a CMDB alive — noticing it has drifted — is automatic and rides the Observation machinery.

### Entity lifecycle: live → retired → pruned

*Agreed 2026-07-22.* Discovery originally only ever created and refreshed entities, so the estate could not tell a live host from a dead one and grew forever on a churning fleet — the other half of the "does it still exist?" question the drift check above only asks about placement.

Every entity carries a **last-seen** stamp, refreshed each time discovery reports it — a distinct fact from "when did anything about it change", because a refresh that finds nothing new is still a sighting. An entity unseen past a window is **retired**: hidden from the estate by default but otherwise whole, its tags, edges, annotations and signal links all intact and still resolving. Discovery reporting it again **un-retires it in place**. That reversibility is what makes the judgement safe to get wrong — an integration having a bad day costs a temporarily-hidden row, not data, which is why retirement rather than deletion does the operator-facing work.

Only after a second, much longer window is a retired entity **pruned**, and pruning refuses anything still bound to an open Incident or carrying a human annotation — authored knowledge is not discovery's to throw away. Its *discovered* dependents go with it; its *history* does not. Signals and playbooks survive with their link nulled, because a record must outlive the thing it was about.

Windows are **per entity type**: transient kinds (hosts, workloads) churn on a timescale structural ones (clusters, namespaces) do not, and one clock for both would either thrash the fleet or never catch the ghosts. Retirement is decided by a slow sweep over last-seen, never inferred from a single sync being complete — one truncated page or degraded source must not retire a slice of the estate.

**Existence and classification read a source's silence differently**, and conflating them is a live bug class. *Any* source naming an entity proves it exists — a monitor scope that merely mentions a host is enough to keep it alive. But only a source that can actually *see* an entity's tags may assert them, so a tag-blind source reporting nothing withdraws nothing. Existence is a low bar that any sighting clears; classification is a claim only an observer may make.

**Maintained through conversation, not forms.** The AI is the interface, over the chat surface:
- **Query** — "what does Kora depend on?", "what runs on g5?" (read-only).
- **Validate** — "is it right that Service-X depends on Database-Z?" — the AI shows its assumption, the human confirms or corrects.
- **Enrich** — "Kora is the CRM, built from these services…" — the AI turns natural language into structured graph updates.

Enrichment is a **write**, but of ISE-internal metadata, not infrastructure — so it does *not* need the tiered-action governance (ADR 0017/0021). It is audited (provenance/authored-by) and confirmed ("I'll record that Kora is the CRM and depends on X/Y/Z — confirm?"): a distinct, lightweight write class kept separate from remediation. Keeping the graph accurate is therefore a conversation, not a chore — which is the real answer to CMDB rot.

**Why it earns its place.** The estate entity id is the **join key across everything** — an Alert's affected entity, an Observation's affected infrastructure (its dedup key), and the evidence gathered during investigation all resolve to the same entity. And during investigation the knowledge base turns blind exploration into **directed, bounded traversal**: resolve the affected entity, walk typed edges to what it runs on / depends on / is depended on by (blast radius), and hand the investigator a scoped evidence plan instead of many exploratory tool round-trips. That directedness is a core part of the cost story.

### The three layers of the estate: Resource, Application, Business Service

*Agreed 2026-08-02 (Sprint 45 design review).* The estate as built is a flat resource inventory — entities of many types, with no statement of what they add up to. Three layers name what an operator actually reasons about, and give an alert somewhere to land that is neither a host nor nothing.

**The layers, and the dependency direction.** Business Service → Application → Resource.

- **Resource** — infrastructure that exists. A server, an RDS instance, an EBS volume, a cluster, a Kubernetes Service, a DNS zone, an EntraID app registration. The physical or virtual compute, network and storage, plus the platform objects that configure them.
- **Application** — a deployed software capability that can be consumed, by a person *or another application*. Chinwag, Kora, an API, a gateway, Twingate. Not touchable, and not the source code that builds it. Defining it by *consumption* rather than by "a thing a user interacts with" is deliberate: a VPN, a DNS service and an API gateway are Applications, and a definition resting on a user interface excludes them.
- **Business Service** — what a customer pays to consume: a telephone answering service, live chat. Notional rather than pointable-at, composed of one or more Applications. **An Application is never a Business Service**, even at one-to-one — collapsing them is what dissolves the model back into a flat list.

**Who owns the truth is what separates the layers.** Resources are **discovered** — an integration enumerates them, refreshes them, and can retire them. Applications and Business Services are **asserted** — no API owns their existence, so a human declares them. That single distinction decides how each layer is maintained and where rot can enter, and it is why the layers are a model rather than a display grouping.

**Environment is part of an Application's identity, not a property of it.** Chinwag in production and Chinwag in test are two Applications (`Chinwag.Prod`, `Chinwag.Test`) — different deployment and change-management processes, sometimes different versions. A **Business Service has no environment of its own**: it composes whichever Application instances are customer-facing, normally Production and also Demo where customers use it. The test is whether a customer touches the environment, not what the environment is called — which makes the boundary auditable rather than a judgement call: a Business Service that has quietly picked up a staging Application becomes a detectable fault, and there are no test Business Services.

**Naming discipline: "service" is always qualified.** The word arrives from every platform meaning something different, so it is never used bare. **Business Service** is the top layer; a **Kubernetes Service** is a Resource; a **DataDog APM Service** is DataDog's view of an Application. Vendor-qualified names need care wherever the vendor name can precede a generic noun — "DataDog Service" is ambiguous between the product and the APM concept, so the product is just **DataDog** (itself an Application ISE does not operate) and the concept is a **DataDog APM Service**.

Existing entity types carrying the collision, to be resolved:
- **`application`** currently holds EntraID **app registrations**, which are identity objects an integration owns — Resources. They become `app-registration`; *Application* is reserved for the middle layer.
- **`service`** holds both DataDog APM services and Kubernetes Services, which sit in different layers and must split.
- **`third-party`** is not a layer. Cloudflare, Twilio and Twingate are Applications; **external-ness is an attribute — who operates it — not a type.**
- **`workload`** is a Resource (settled below).

**Each integration declares what it is a source of record for.** AWS for AWS resources, Kubernetes for cluster objects, EntraID for identity objects, Status Pages for external Applications — because nothing else will ever tell ISE that Twilio exists. **DataDog is a source of record for nothing**, and neither is Freshservice: DataDog holds Monitors and Alerts, and neither is a thing in the estate. Its identifiers become **aliases on entities other sources own**, never entities in their own right. This is the observation-versus-record line — an observation source knows only what happens to be instrumented and reporting, which is a partial and fragmented view of the world, and promoting that to a statement about what *exists* is what fills an estate with opaquely-named half-entities (246 DataDog hosts, 73 of them named by raw instance id or UUID, at the time this was agreed).

**Unknown assets.** When an alert arrives against something no source of record claims, the alert and any Incident are still created and flagged as an **unknown asset** — but **no placeholder entity is minted**, which is the pollution this section exists to design out. The flag is a temporary state, not a label: when the account, cluster or server is finally integrated, the existing back-fill re-links the alert to the real Resource with its incident history intact. Gaps are deduplicated by the identifier the source used ("4 unknown hosts, 17 alerts", never 17 orphans), which turns the integration backlog into an evidence-backed list — and later becomes the detector for infrastructure appearing outside the accounts ISE knows about.

**Terminology.** *Entity* remains the technical umbrella in the model, the API and the code; user-facing surfaces use the layer names now that those are precise. A generic word is genuinely needed in exactly one place — an alert whose subject cannot be identified — and there "unknown asset" reads plainly.

**Mapping is a tagging exercise.** Membership of the layers above the Resource line is expressed by tags resolving through the Tag Dictionary, which makes the mapping exercise and the tag-hygiene exercise the same work and reuses the groups machinery. Two consequences to hold on to: a Resource serving several Applications means **Application→Resource is a relationship, not an attribute** — a tag can seed it, it cannot express many, and the payoff is that a shared database going red is three Business Services impacted, with severity becoming contextual rather than fixed. And a fleet-wide monitor ("high CPU on any VM") has no subject as a *rule* while every *alert* it fires does, so resolution matches on what the alert fired **about**, not what the monitor is scoped to; an alert that genuinely names no subject belongs to the integration or a group, and saying so is honest rather than a failure.

**A Kubernetes workload is a Resource.** *(Settled 2026-08-02.)* The case for promoting it to an Application instance rests on a one-to-one correspondence that does not exist: an Application is realistically several workloads — a web deployment, a worker, a scheduler, possibly the same set again in a second region — so workloads map **many-to-one** onto Applications, and promoting them would still require an Application concept above to group them. That builds the middle layer anyway *and* mislabels the Resource layer. Two supporting reasons: a cluster is full of workloads that are plainly not Applications (cert-manager, external-secrets, agents, operators), and deciding which of them count is itself a human assertion; and promoting them would make Applications discoverable on Kubernetes but asserted on VMs — one Application with two provenances, one of which the retirement sweep could quietly delete.

**Applications are seeded by one proposal each, not one per workload.** "Workloads tagged `app:chinwag env:prod` suggest an Application `Chinwag.Prod` composed of these three" — a single confirmation covering many Resources, with membership then following the tags as they change. The payoff is stability: workloads recycle constantly and the Application does not, which makes it the right anchor for the things that must attach to the most stable subject available — incident memory, playbooks, annotations — and the layer a DataDog APM Service and a synthetic monitor both resolve onto.

Two consequences settled with it:
- **The Application→Resource edge needs its own type.** It is *composition*, not placement. Reusing `runs-on` would inherit that type's exclusion from drift checks and from the impact walk, silently breaking the very rollup the layer exists to provide.
- **Applications and Business Services are never retired by discovery** — the same treatment `group` has today. If every workload under an Application vanishes that is an Observation, not a retirement; their lifecycle belongs to the assertion that created them.

## Integrations & Connectors

An **Integration Type** is a connector — the code that teaches ISE to work with a kind of system. A **developer** builds and tests it; it declares a set of capabilities and is released like any other code. An **Integration** is a configured instance of a Type: an **admin** supplies credentials and connects it to a specific target (this cluster, this DataDog account) and sets instance options (Obs Loop schedule, scoped severity overrides). **Users** then interact with what it surfaces. The bright line: **anything with a coding element is a developer's job; instantiating a tested Type is an admin's; everything else is a user's** — no muddy middle. In today's code these are `connector_type` (the registered class) and `System` (the configured instance).

**Capabilities (declared, optional).** A Type declares which it provides, and the platform degrades gracefully by capability:
- **Alerts** — poll the source's own detection layer → Alert signals (native levels mapped onto the canonical ladder).
- **Observations** — run ISE's detectors where the source has no detection layer → Observation signals (severity + confidence).
- **Entities** — enumerate entities, their native keys and observable edges → estate knowledge base.
- **Evidence** — on-demand read queries during investigation.
- **Actions** — the governed remediation catalogue (`ActionSpec`, with tier and reversibility).
- **Documents** — fetch registered narrative documents from their source of truth (see *The Document Register*).
- plus lifecycle: credential spec, health check.

DataDog declares Alerts + Entities + Evidence + Actions (it detects for itself); Kubernetes declares Observations + Entities + Evidence + Actions (no detection layer); Confluence declares Documents alone. A source may declare *both* Alerts and Observations (e.g. AWS: CloudWatch alarms + raw config).

**Two kinds of capability, two kinds of reuse.** *Semantic* capabilities (Alerts/Observations/Entities) map source concepts onto ISE's canonical model — reuse is a shared contract shape plus a small per-source mapping, not shared code. *Tool* capabilities (Evidence, Actions, Documents) are "give the AI access" and can be provided generically — e.g. a generic MCP-backed Type. Governing principle: **the more governance a capability needs, the less generic it can be** — read-only Evidence generalises cheapest (and fits the ADR 0023 read-only boundary); governed Actions carry irreducible per-source classification (the `ActionSpec` tier/reversibility). Reuse in practice: one Kubernetes Type serves any cluster (each cluster is an Integration); a monitoring Type shares a capability *shape* across vendors with thin per-vendor mapping; a generic MCP Type provides Evidence (and, with per-action classification, Actions) for many sources.

**End-state: integrations as independently-deployable modules.** The target is for an Integration Type to be an **out-of-process service behind a versioned contract**, deployed and removed on its own cadence (its own Helm chart / immutable image) without a core release — the registry becomes a runtime registry of *endpoints*, not import-time classes. MCP is the ready-made mechanism for the tool capabilities (Evidence/Actions), directly realising ADR 0014; the semantic capabilities need a richer versioned contract and are more work. Two non-negotiables keep this real, not illusory: integrations talk to core **only through the versioned contract, never the shared database** (or schema changes force plugin releases); and **authorization stays in core** — a plugin executes an already-approved action, it never self-authorises (preserving ADR 0017/0021). The permanent tax is **contract versioning** — "add without a core release" holds exactly as long as the contract is stable. This is a platform-extensibility goal, downstream of the cost-critical work, and staged: MCP tool-capabilities first, the semantic-capability contract later.

## Knowledge sources & authority (Relationship Mapping)

*Added 2026-07-22 (Sprint 20 design). How the knowledge base's facts come to exist, how much each is trusted, and how conflict and decay are handled.*

**The four sources.** Everything ISE knows about the estate arrives through one of four sources, ordered by how rigid they are:

1. **Declared — tags.** Standardised key attributes stated at source (`env:prod`, `service:kora`, `role:database`) by whoever configured the thing. Authoritative in intent and self-maintaining (sync set-replacement withdraws what a source stops asserting), but only as consistent as the humans typing them — `env:prod` / `env:Prod` / `env:production` coexist today. The **Tag Dictionary** (below) is what makes them the fixed points of truth they should be.
2. **Deduced — integration structure.** What ISE works out from knowing how a platform actually works: pod-1 runs in namespace-a; a Service's selector routes to its workloads. Deterministic, refreshed by discovery, drift-checked. Depth honestly varies by integration: Kubernetes is rich; DataDog is a flat list whose structure must be recovered from its own tags (`kube_node:` → node cross-keys) and, where APM exists, the service-dependency map. Connectors declare what they can deduce (capability contract, ADR 0027).
3. **Learned — incident experience.** Causal knowledge found by working incidents ("this RDS instance broke, which took the Kora web UI down"). Captured at the Incident Loop's Update step as *proposed* structure (`depends-on`, resolution `ai_proposed`) that a human confirms — never auto-asserted; one incident is weak evidence, recurrence strengthens. Learned edges attach to the most stable subject available (the service, not the transient node it happened to be on).
4. **Authored — narrative context.** Human-provided documentation: annotations today, plus the **Document Register** (below) — live documents (runbooks, architecture notes) registered from their source of truth and scraped on a cadence. Consumed twice: as summarised AI investigation context, and mined into *proposed* structured claims that go through the confirm pipeline. A document is a source like any other — its extracted claims carry its provenance and are withdrawn or re-reviewed when it changes or disappears. Its *statements* are unverified narrative until confirmed, which is why documents sit lowest in authority even though the scrape keeps their freshness known.

**Everything is a claim with provenance.** Every fact — a tag on an entity, an edge, a group membership, an annotation, a statement extracted from a document — is a claim carrying *source* (which integration / document / incident / person), *method* (harvested / deduced / rule / ai_proposed / asserted) and *freshness* (first seen, last confirmed). This exists in fragments already (edge/alias `resolution`, `entity_tag.system_id`, `last_confirmed_at`, entity last-seen per ISE-206); Relationship Mapping generalises the model rather than inventing per-source machinery.

**Authority = method × freshness.** When claims conflict, ISE uses this order, freshness modulating within a tier:

1. **Human-asserted** — sticky; a fresh observed contradiction surfaces for re-confirmation, it never silently overrides a human.
2. **Currently-observed declared & deduced facts** (tags, discovery) — self-refreshing, so currency is knowable.
3. **Rule-derived** (groups) — deterministic consequences of tier 2.
4. **Incident-learned proposals** — until confirmed (confirmation promotes them to tier 1).
5. **Document-stated** — until confirmed.

So a current hard tag ("host xyz is the database") beats a stale document ("the app database is called abc") — by construction, not by special-casing.

**Conflicts are surfaced, never silently resolved.** Authority decides what ISE *uses*; the disagreement itself is kept and shown — a conflict flag on the entity, and for material drift an Observation (the existing drift machinery). Verifiability is provenance made visible: every fact rendered in the UI shows who says so, by what method, and when last confirmed — that is how a second person checks it is correct.

**Decay and pruning ride the source.** Each claim is withdrawn by the thing that made it: sync set-replacement for tags; discovery refresh plus the entity lifecycle (retire → prune, ISE-206) for deduced structure; document change/removal for extracted claims; recency-weighting for incident memory. Human assertions persist until a human retracts them (audited). Nothing lingers without a live source or a human signature behind it.

### The Tag Dictionary

*Agreed 2026-07-22.* The governance layer that turns raw tags into a standard vocabulary. It is to tags what aliases are to entities: many raw names, one canonical identity, explicit provenance on the link.

- **Standard keys.** A defined list of canonical keys (`env`, `service`, `cluster`, `role`, `team`, …), each with **aliases** (`env` ← `Env`, `environment`) and a **description** — the description exists for the AI as much as for people: it is how the investigator knows what `role:` means in this estate.
- **Values are `defined` or `open`.** A defined key carries a standard value list, each value with its own aliases (`prod` ← `Prod`, `production`) and description; an open key (`service:`) accepts any value, synonyms still mappable. Users can add to, edit, and delete from the standard lists — the dictionary is theirs, not shipped dogma.
- **Mapping, never rewriting.** Raw tags stay in the pool exactly as reported; a mapped tag points at its canonical (`canonical_id`, the entity-alias pattern). All matching — tag rules, groups, Tag Cloud heat, impact rollups — resolves through the canonical; all display shows the canonical with the original in the provenance tooltip ("`env:prod` — reported as `env:Prod` by DataDog"). Unmapping is a clean undo.
- **Dictionary aliases apply deterministically at ingest** — an alias listed in the dictionary *is* the confirmed mapping, so it resolves without ceremony. Only the truly weird (`pr0d`) escalates: a deterministic detector (case-fold, punctuation variants, seeded synonym table) plus AI for the long tail proposes candidates into the proposals queue. Nothing unlisted maps silently. A confirmed mapping is an audited human assertion about vocabulary; confirming one re-evaluates rules and groups deterministically.
- **Coverage expectations** per key declare which entity types should carry it ("hosts and workloads carry `env`"). The **compliance report** shows coverage %, values resolving only via alias, and values that exist in no dictionary — per integration, so "DataDog hosts are 40% missing `env`" is visible, assignable work. Coverage is measured over the entities an integration *knows* (its aliases), never the entities it has *tagged* — the latter drops the untagged host from its own denominator and reports 100% while half the fleet is unlabelled, which is the exact failure the report exists to catch. ISE never writes tags back to the source; fix-at-source proposals through the governed Actions layer are a recorded future model (ISE-210), not in scope.
- **Screens:** dictionary editor + review queue in Settings → Tags (admin); compliance/hygiene view on the Tag Cloud page (all users).
- **Seeded, then sanitised.** Ships seeded with the known keys and common synonym sets, and with value mappings proposed from the estate's actual pool; cleaned up in use.

### The Document Register

*Agreed 2026-07-22; built ISE-219/220, ADR 0042.* Narrative documentation as a first-class, synced knowledge source — the fourth source made real.

- **The source of truth stays where it lives; ISE scrapes it.** Live documents are kept in Confluence. A document is registered in ISE by **URL + plain-text description + tag list**, and ISE fetches it on a regular cadence — nobody has to remember to copy documentation in after editing it. The scrape gives documents what tags and discovery already have: ISE knows when a document changed and when it disappeared. (Confluence is the first document source; the fetcher is a connector **Documents** capability, so other sources can follow.)
- **Tags link documents to the estate.** The document's tag list resolves through the Tag Dictionary to the entities and groups carrying those tags — tag a runbook `service:kora` and it rides along wherever Kora is investigated, with no manual entity links to rot. There is deliberately **no document→entity link at all**: a hand-made one is correct on the day it is made and rots silently after, where a tag-resolved one follows the estate as the estate changes. The description tells the AI (and people) what the document is for.
- **Consumed twice.** (a) *As context:* the AI maintains a short structured summary per document, refreshed when the scrape sees a change; summaries ride into investigations touching a linked entity by default, and a `read_document` tool pulls full text on demand — spend proportional to need, the evidence-on-demand philosophy. (b) *As claims:* on change, extraction proposes structured facts (edges, tags, aliases) into the proposals queue, each citing the passage it came from — confirming is reading a sentence, not a runbook.
- **Change is what costs, and change is defined narrowly.** The summary and the extraction both run *only* when a re-read finds the content actually different — never on a clock — so an estate that never edits its runbooks pays nothing after the first scrape. "Different" means the text, not the source's version token: a version bump with identical prose is a re-render, not an edit, and treating it as one would buy a paid re-summary on every no-op save.
- **Lifecycle rides the scrape.** Content change → summary and extraction re-run and *diff*: statements no longer made are withdrawn (unconfirmed) or flagged for re-review (confirmed — a human signature survives its source, never silently). Page gone → the same rule applied to the whole document at once. The source's own last-modified feeds freshness; a long-unchanged document is presented to the AI as aged, not as current truth.
- **Only a successful read may withdraw anything.** A failed fetch or a failed extraction diffs nothing at all. Treating "the source was unreachable" or "the model was unavailable" as "the document no longer says this" would quietly withdraw the estate's claims on an outage — the highest-consequence mistake available here, and the reason a deleted page (a fact about the source) and a timeout (a fact about the network) are distinguished rather than merged.
- **A document cannot invent infrastructure.** Extracted claims are resolved against entities the estate already knows; a claim naming something unknown, or a name two entities share, is dropped rather than guessed. The queue is only worth reading if everything in it is about something real.

### The proposals queue

*Agreed 2026-07-22.* One place where "ISE thinks it learned something — confirm?" lands, regardless of source: tag-mapping candidates (Dictionary), document-extracted claims, incident-learned edges, AI identity matches. Filterable by source; one-click confirm/reject with the citation or evidence alongside; **its own nav entry** — it is operator work, not admin configuration. Confirmation is the act that promotes a claim to a tier-1 human assertion; rejection is remembered so the same proposal does not return.

A claim is identified by **its own content, not by the row** — so the same claim raised twice strengthens one queue item instead of opening a second, and a claim already settled is never re-asked. That single choice is what buys detector idempotency and durable rejection together.

The queue answers a second question for document claims: not "is this true?" but **"is this still true?"** — a confirmed claim whose source stopped saying it is flagged for re-review rather than withdrawn. The only answer offered inline is *still true*; retracting means removing the edge or tag the confirmation created, and that belongs on the entity's own screen where the reviewer can see what else it touches.

### Impact & what-if

*Agreed 2026-07-22.* The rollup lands on the **incident "Affects" panel first** — the screen where "which Services does this hit? prod or test?" is actually asked — with the entity page as the second view of the same data. Both are views over one deterministic reverse traversal of the durable edge graph (walk incoming `depends-on` / `routes-to` from the affected entity), so the speculative form — **"what would happen if X failed?"** — comes almost free: the same query bound to any entity, as an impact preview on the entity page and as a chat question the AI narrates over. No AI is needed for the answer itself.

**Containment is not consequence**, and the walk must not conflate them: a node failing takes the workloads on it, but a workload failing does not take the node, so `runs-on` and `part-of` are excluded from the upward walk. Groups are read separately, as context rather than as blast radius.

Storage doctrine restated for this: every fact is a durable row — entities, edges, tags, documents, extracted claims. A confirmed document-derived edge is an ordinary `entity_edge` row distinguished only by provenance; unconfirmed proposals never join the graph. A what-if view may show unconfirmed proposals as visually-distinct hints ("a runbook claims billing-api also uses this — unconfirmed"), never mixed in as fact — the conflicts-surfaced principle applied to absence.

## Webhook Events (pushed context)

Every other integration is one ISE *pulls* from; Webhook Events are the reverse — an external system *pushes* a fact at ISE. A known event happens in the world (a release ships, a change is applied, a job runs) and the system that did it tells ISE, rather than ISE discovering it.

Decisions (Sprint 22 planning, 2026-07-25):

- **A fifth information source.** Webhook events sit alongside the four existing analysis sources — synced connector state, native signals, the estate knowledge base, and on-demand evidence — answering the question none of them can: *what did someone deliberately change, and when?* Motivating scenario: an alert fires for Chinwag crashing; analysis surfaces the Chinwag release event received an hour earlier.
- **Sources are pre-registered.** A webhook source is created in Settings and issued its own secret-token endpoint (Slack-style unique URL per source). Per-source identity, revocation and rotation; no open endpoint, no shared key.
- **ISE defines the schema.** Senders conform to a small ISE event schema (title, level, event type, outcome, status, alert key, entity hint, timestamp, detail) — predictable for the Events screen and for AI consumption — with the raw payload preserved verbatim. Adapters for vendor formats are a possible later addition, not the native model.
- **Both wirings into analysis.** A bounded recent-events summary is auto-included in incident analysis (events touching the affected entity's names/aliases first), and a read-only lookup tool lets investigation surfaces dig further on demand.
- **Events are data, never instructions.** The same untrusted-content posture as evidence and documents: event content informs, it cannot direct.
- **Visibility is first-class.** Events get their own top-level screen (nav entry, list + detail) — the pane-of-glass surface, not just an AI input.
- **Levelled events; alert-level events raise Signals.** *(Agreed 2026-07-25.)* Every event carries a `level` (info / warn / alert). Info and warn are pure events; an alert-level event *additionally* raises an ordinary Alert signal — the sender is a source with its own detection layer, so ISE forwards its verdict, per the deferral principle. This is the cheap path to DataDog-style alerting for systems with no supported integration. The per-source level→ladder mapping, the severity-override layer and the global auto-incident threshold apply unchanged — no new promotion machinery. Identity is `source + alert_key` (schema field, defaulting to event type) so a re-fire attaches rather than duplicates; an optional entity hint resolves against estate aliases (matched → the signal joins the graph; unmatched → a signal without an entity, never an invented one).
- **Recovery is told or timed, never observed.** The push-source problem: ISE cannot poll the sender to watch an alert go green. A `status: recovered` event with the same `alert_key` clears the alert, and a per-source TTL fallback auto-recovers alerts from fire-and-forget senders after a window of silence — both, because the sender population spans smart and simple.

## The AI interaction model: three pillars

*Agreed 2026-07-25 (Sprint 24 — AI Interaction Review; ISE-263 map / ISE-264 audit / ISE-265 catalogue, plus the requirements discussion). The judging frame is the operator's: cost vs benefit per incident. The failures found: AI spend scaled with estate size rather than incident difficulty, and the surface with dialogue (chat) was denied reach while the surface with reach (diagnose) had no dialogue.*

1. **Conversation-first investigation.** Issue-chat is the primary investigation surface: it can look anywhere read-only (stored state, estate graph, on-demand Evidence), the operator steers it mid-stream, and it commits structured findings to the record. A diagnosis is what a good investigation session *produces* — the `commit_diagnosis` route — with the single-shot diagnose run retained as the one-click convenience, not the primary path.
2. **Cost proportional to difficulty.** Every question starts with the cheapest sufficient answer and escalates only when inconclusive. Self-resolution — the common case — is deterministic before it is agentic: "has the signal recovered?" is answered from the DB with zero model tokens (cheap-verdict-first). Context is **pulled, not pushed**: no force-fed estate block; the agent fetches the bounded, hub-summarised neighbourhood when the question needs it. Run token caps are runaway guards, not cost controls — the fix for a run hitting the cap is to shrink what it assembles and re-carries, never to raise the cap; per-task-type caps are set from measured numbers (per-stage token instrumentation), a recheck never sharing a full investigation's budget. The guard counts **fresh tokens** (`total − cache_read`): cached re-carry is the legitimate, cheap bulk of a tool loop, so only fresh assembly reads as runaway (ISE-294).
3. **Zero-cost retrieval.** The mechanical work of surfacing relevant information must not cost AI tokens. The retrieval contract — and the integration contract for every future source (webhooks, IAC repos): **comprehend once at write time** (cheap-to-read forms computed at ingest — the document-summary pattern generalised); **find in the database** (indexed, ranked search as deterministic tool calls); **read shortlists** (small, truncation-honest results the model drills into by choice). The model's budget is spent reasoning about evidence, never locating it.

Boundary decision recorded with the pillars: **the chat boundary is a WRITE boundary** (amends ADR 0023's framing). Chat may not mutate ISE or the estate — enforced by the read-only DB session — but read-only Evidence pulls are permitted (side-effect-free by contract, ADR 0031), and chat may write to ISE's own record in the governed loop-driver pattern (messages, enqueued runs, committed diagnoses). Nothing here weakens the hard line: no agent, on any surface, ever executes a mutation.

## Why it's shaped this way

Detection already exists inside the sources, so ISE forwards Alerts cheaply and deterministically. ISE spends real compute only where it must — on Observations, on a slow loop — and only on confirmed signal, inside an Incident investigation a human cares about. Competence comes overwhelmingly from what ISE builds — the knowledge base, methods (playbooks) and memory — not from the raw model or integrations; and that scaffolding is simultaneously the quality play and the cost fix, because good context and method let a cheaper model do less work for a better answer. The design keeps the expensive, AI-driven work off idle timers and pointed at things that are actually happening.

## Delivery philosophy

Design the whole target before building, so the data model and capabilities are right and **nothing has to be torn up and rebuilt**. Deliver it incrementally, but only in slices that are *permanent parts of the final thing* — never throwaway scaffolding a later phase supersedes. Where feedback matters (and for a product proving its own viability, it does), deliver the **load-bearing spine first** — for the knowledge base, that is entities + aliases + resolve-during-investigation — because the remaining registers (context, tuning), drift detection and conversational enrichment all hang off that spine additively.
